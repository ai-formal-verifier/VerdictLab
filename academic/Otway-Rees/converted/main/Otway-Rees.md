**Editorial note — one sentence in §4 disambiguated by this project.** The original read "B forwards both to the server, who decrypts and checks whether the components M, A, and B match in the encrypted messages", which leaves implicit which keys S decrypts with and can be misread as "the two ciphertexts match each other". It now reads "who looks up the principals A and B named in the request, extracting their respective keys K_as and K_bs, decrypts, and checks whether the components M, A, and B inside each encrypted message match the plaintext M, A, B of the request" — the inner identifiers must equal the outer plaintext identifiers S used to look the keys up. The key lookup is stated the way server-based standards state it; Kerberos (RFC 4120 §3.1.3) has the server look up "the client and server principals named in the KRB_AS_REQ in its database, extracting their respective keys". Without the fix an extractor could have S compare only the two ciphertexts' contents without binding them to the outer identities, which would let an attacker have S encrypt the session key under a key of its choosing.

## 1  Introduction

The Otway-Rees protocol is a key exchange protocol designed to allow two parties (A and B) to establish a secure session key (K_ab) through a trusted third-party authentication server (S). Unlike the Needham-Schroeder protocol, Otway-Rees does not rely on synchronized clocks, instead using nonces (random numbers) to ensure freshness and prevent replay attacks.

## 2  Protocol Participants

- **A (Initiator)**: The party who initiates the authentication request.
- **B (Responder)**: The party with whom A wishes to communicate.
- **S**: A trusted third party that shares a symmetric master key with both A and B.

## 3  Notation

- **A, B**: Identities of the two principals.
- **S**: The trusted authentication server.
- **M**: A common nonce (session identifier) generated by A.
- **N_a, N_b**: Unique nonces generated by A and B respectively to ensure freshness.
- **K_as**: Symmetric key shared between A and S.
- **K_bs**: Symmetric key shared between B and S.
- **K_ab**: The new session key generated by S for use between A and B.
- **{...}K**: Information encrypted using the symmetric key K.

## 4  Protocol Description

Otway and Rees proposed a shared-key authentication protocol which involves two principals and an
authentication server (1987). The protocol is attractive in that it provides good timeliness
guarantees in a small number of messages. Moreover, it makes no use of synchronized clocks,
and is easily implemented as two nested remote procedure calls. Although not widely used,
it is a well designed protocol that may have application in certain environments.

We give the protocol below, with A and B as the two principals, K_as and K_bs as their private keys,
S as the authentication server. The principals A and B generate the nonces N_a, N_b, and M; the server
S generates K_ab, which becomes the session key between A and B.

Message 1 A -> B: M, A, B, {N_a, M, A, B}K_as  
Message 2 B -> S: M, A, B, {N_a, M, A, B}K_as, {N_b, M, A, B}K_bs  
Message 3 S -> B: M, {N_a, K_ab}K_as, {N_b, K_ab}K_bs  
Message 4 B -> A: M, {N_a, K_ab}K_as

A passes to B some encrypted material useful only to the server, together with enough information for B
to make up a similar encrypted message. B forwards both to the server, who looks up the principals A and B named in the request, extracting their respective keys K_as and K_bs, decrypts, and checks whether
the components M, A, and B inside each encrypted message match the plaintext M, A, B of the request. If so, S generates K_ab and embeds it in
two encrypted messages, one for each participant, accompanied by the appropriate nonces. Both are sent
to B, who forwards the appropriate part to A. Then A and B decrypt, check their nonces, and if satisfied proceed to use K_ab.

## 5  Assumptions

This section defines the complete object-level assumption space for
the Section 6 goals.

- **Open peer admission.** The identity scope `peer_auth` is fixed open. The
  attacker may register a recognised peer identity that honest principals can
  select. This condition applies to every scenario.
- **Disclosure of A's long-term key.** `K_as` is a variable data-exposure
  dimension with a normal value in which the attacker does not obtain it and a
  compromise value in which it is disclosed from the beginning of the run.
- **Disclosure of B's long-term key.** `K_bs` is an independent variable
  data-exposure dimension with the same two values: not obtained, or disclosed
  from the beginning of the run.

The analysis uses the baseline, disclosure of `K_as` alone, and disclosure of
`K_bs` alone.

## 6  Security Properties

Exactly five goals are posed for formal verification.

- **Session-key secrecy.** `K_ab` is secret from the attacker and may be known
  only by the principals playing `A` and `B`.
- **A-to-B identity agreement.** `A` is the asserter and `B` is the subject of
  injective agreement on `(A, B)`.
- **A-to-B key agreement.** `A` is the asserter and `B` is the subject of
  injective agreement on `(A, B, K_ab)`.
- **B-to-A identity agreement.** `B` is the asserter and `A` is the subject of
  injective agreement on `(A, B)`.
- **B-to-A key agreement.** `B` is the asserter and `A` is the subject of
  injective agreement on `(A, B, K_ab)`.

Session-key secrecy is evaluated in the baseline and in both long-term-key
disclosure scenarios. The four injective-agreement goals are evaluated only in
the baseline. Open admission applies throughout.
