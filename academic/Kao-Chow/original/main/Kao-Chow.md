## 1 Introduction

The **Kao-Chow Protocol** is a mutual authentication and key distribution protocol designed for distributed systems 
using symmetric cryptography. It was developed to address specific vulnerabilities in earlier protocols (like the 
Needham-Schroeder protocol) while eliminating the requirement for **synchronized clocks** across a network.

The protocol relies on a trusted third-party **Authentication Server (S)** to generate a session key and distribute 
it to two principals, **A** and **B**. By employing a dual-nonce challenge-response mechanism, the protocol ensures t
hat both parties can verify the freshness of the session key and the identity of the peer, effectively preventing 
replay attacks even if the responder (B) cannot initially verify the server's message.

## 2 Protocol Participants

The protocol involves three distinct entities:

- **Principal A (Initiator):** The user or system that initiates the communication request to talk to B.
- **Principal B (Responder):** The target peer with whom A wishes to establish a secure channel.
- **Authentication Server S (Key Distribution Center):** A trusted authority that shares a long-term master key with every registered principal. S is responsible for generating and securely distributing the session key K_ab.

## 3 Notation

The following notation is used to describe the message flow and cryptographic operations:
- **A, B, S**: The identities of Principal A, Principal B, and the Server S.
- **N_a, N_b**: Nonces (random numbers) generated by A and B respectively to ensure freshness.
- **K_as**: The master key shared between Principal A and Server S.
- **K_bs**: The master key shared between Principal B and Server S.
- **K_ab**: The new session key generated by S for use between A and B.
- **{M}K**: A message **M** encrypted using the symmetric key **K**.
- **X → Y: M**: Principal **X** sends message **M** to Principal **Y**.

## 4 The Proposed Nonce-based Protocol

The assumptions of the environment where the protocol is to be operated and of possible attacks are basically the 
same as those assumed by most existing authentication protocols. Two principals, A and B, wishing to authenticate 
each other and to obtain a shared session key for subsequent communication. A trusted authentication server S 
shares a master key with each principal and is capable of producing good session keys and sending them securely 
on the requests of principals. No clock synchronization among machines is assumed, so nonce-based challenge/response 
exchanges are used to guarantee the freshness of messages.

The message flow of the protocol and the contents of each message are as follows:
* **Message 1** A → S: A, B, N_a 
* **Message 2** S → B: {A, B, N_a, K_ab}K_as, {A, B, N_a, K_ab}K_bs 
* **Message 3** B → A: {A, B, N_a, K_ab}K_as, {N_a}K_ab, N_b 
* **Message 4** A → B: {N_b}K_ab

Principal A initiates the authentication by sending S a plaintext message containing the identities of itself and the 
desired communicating peer B, and a nonce N_a (message 1). After S receives this message, he generates a session key 
K_ab and appends it to the identities of both parties and nonce N_a to form two credentials, one for A and the other 
for B. Both credentials have exactly the same contents, but one is encrypted with A's master key K_as, and the other 
is encrypted with B's master key K_bs.

S sends both credentials to B (message 2), who then decrypts the second one and finds out that A wants to authenticate 
with B mutually, N_a is the nonce issued by A, and K_ab is generated by S to be used as a session key for future 
communication between A and B. B then forwards the first credential from S to A, and also sends an encrypted N_a 
with K_ab and another nonce N_b (message 3).  

Upon receiving them, A decrypts the credential to get K_ab and verifies its freshness by checking the presence of N_a. 
A also authenticates B by decrypting the encrypted part with K_ab and comparing the result with N_a. If they match, 
A encrypts N_b with K_ab and sends it back to B (message 4) to prove its identity to B.  

In the protocol, A verifies the identity of B by checking whether the peer principal is able to encrypt nonce N_a with 
session key K_ab. This verification is based upon two beliefs of A. The first one is that on the request of authentication 
(message 1), S will issue a credential containing N_a and K_ab and encrypted with K_bs for principal B (message 2). The 
second belief of A is that only S and B share master key K_bs, so no other principal except B is able to send the encrypted 
N_a with K_ab (message 3). Therefore, the protocol prevents against impersonation of B by the assumptions of the correct 
behavior of the authentication server and of the secrecy of master keys. Furthermore, since nonce N_a is used only for the 
current session, replay of old messages issued by either S or B will be detected.

On the other side, B verifies the identity of A by the use of uncertified session keys. When B receives message 2, he 
has no way to tell whether the message is either a replay or an impersonation attempt initiated by a malicious principal 
C. B only presumes that some principal who claims to be A wants to authenticate each other with himself for the current
session. To verify message 2 is authentic and fresh, B needs to use (and temporarily believes) the uncertified session 
key K_ab to encrypt N_a and also sends its own nonce N_b in the clear.

If A returns message 4 as expected, B believes in the authenticity and freshness of K_ab. If message 2 is only a replay 
(either copy-and-replay or suppress-and-play), A will detect it and thus will not respond with a normal message 4 
(instead, A probably sends back an error message to inform B that a replay is possibly occurring), so B knows K_ab 
is not fresh. If principal C wants to impersonate A and initiates an authentication process, he is incapable of 
producing message 4 since K_ab is confidential to C. Therefore, B can verify its temporal belief on the authenticity 
and freshness of message 2 by a nonce challenge/response exchange with A. Note that B authenticates A basing upon the 
beliefs similar to those on which A bases to authenticate B.

## 5 Assumptions

This section defines the complete assumption space for the Section 6 goals.
The public attacker-controlled network and unbounded sessions are already the
flow-model defaults and are not separate entries.

- **Disclosure of A's long-term key.** `K_as` is an independent data-exposure
  dimension. The normal value leaves `K_as` unavailable to the attacker; the
  compromise value discloses `K_as` to the attacker from the beginning of the
  run.
- **Disclosure of B's long-term key.** `K_bs` is a separate data-exposure
  dimension. The normal value leaves `K_bs` unavailable to the attacker; the
  compromise value discloses `K_bs` to the attacker from the beginning of the
  run.

The analysis uses the baseline, disclosure of `K_as` alone, and disclosure of
`K_bs` alone. It does not introduce a separate exposure dimension for `K_ab`.

## 6 Security Properties

The following five goals are posed for formal verification. All agreement
goals use the same tuple `(A, B, N_a, N_b, K_ab)`.

- **Session-key secrecy.** `K_ab` is secret from the attacker and may be known
  only by `A`, `B`, and `S`.
- **B's non-injective agreement with A.** `B` is the asserter and `A` is the
  subject of non-injective agreement on `(A, B, N_a, N_b, K_ab)`.
- **A's non-injective agreement with B.** `A` is the asserter and `B` is the
  subject of non-injective agreement on `(A, B, N_a, N_b, K_ab)`.
- **B's injective agreement with A.** `B` is the asserter and `A` is the
  subject of injective agreement on `(A, B, N_a, N_b, K_ab)`.
- **A's injective agreement with B.** `A` is the asserter and `B` is the
  subject of injective agreement on `(A, B, N_a, N_b, K_ab)`.

Session-key secrecy, B's non-injective agreement, and B's injective agreement
are evaluated in the baseline and in both long-term-key disclosure scenarios.
A's non-injective and injective agreement goals are evaluated only in the
baseline. Being evaluated does not imply that a goal holds.
