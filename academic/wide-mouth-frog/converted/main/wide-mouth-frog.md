**Editorial note — message-type value in §4 added by this project.** [1] states, as a general assumption for every protocol it analyses, that each principal can detect and ignore its own messages, and leaves the information that makes this possible out of its examples for brevity; the two message lines of §4 are written that way. §4 now makes that information explicit as a message-type value inside each encrypted part (1 for Message 1, 2 for Message 2), checked by S and by B. Deployed protocols keep one encrypted part from being taken for another in the same way: Kerberos binds every encrypted part to its own key usage value (RFC 4120 §4, §7.5.1). Without it, S would accept its own Message 2 as a new Message 1 and could be driven to re-timestamp a key indefinitely — the replay [2] records from [3], which the comment by Abadi in [2] says works only against weakened variants of the protocol.

## 1  Introduction

The **Wide-mouthed-frog protocol** is a shared-key authentication protocol characterized by its extreme simplicity.
It was proposed by Michael Burrows (M.B.) and stands as perhaps the simplest method for key distribution involving
an authentication server. Unlike more complex protocols that use nonces for freshness, this protocol relies on
**synchronized clocks** to guarantee timeliness.

## 2  Protocol Participants

The protocol involves three distinct principals:

- **A (Alice)**: The initiator who, in this specific protocol, chooses the session key himself/herself.
- **B (Bob)**: The recipient of the session key.
- **S (Server)**: A trusted authentication server that shares a secret key with both A and B.

## 3  Notion

To analyze the protocol within the logic of authentication, the following notation is used:  
**A, B, S**: Specific principals involved in the exchange.  
**K_as, K_bs**: Shared keys between the principals and the server.  
**K_ab**: The session key chosen by A for communication with B.  
**T_a, T_s**: Timestamps generated by A and S respectively.  
**{X}\_K**: Formula X encrypted under key K.

## 4  Protocol Description

The following unpublished protocol was proposed by one of us (M.B.), and is perhaps the simplest protocol that
uses shared-key cryptography and an authentication server. It transfers a key from A to B via S in only two messages
by using synchronized clocks, and by allowing A to choose the session key:  
Message 1 A -> S: A, {T_a, B, K_ab}K_as  
Message 2 S -> B: {T_s, A, K_ab}K_bs

Each encrypted part begins with a message-type value that tells the two messages apart, so the encrypted parts are
{1, T_a, B, K_ab}K_as and {2, T_s, A, K_ab}K_bs. S accepts Message 1 only when the decrypted type is 1, and B accepts
Message 2 only when it is 2.

A sends a session key to S, including a timestamp T_a.
S checks that the first message is timely, and if it is, it forwards the message to B, together with its own timestamp T_s.
B then checks that the timestamp from S is later than any other it has received from S.
Since all timestamps are either checked or generated by S, each principal need only record the difference between
his own clock and S's clock, updating it whenever clock drift causes an authentication message to be rejected.

## 5  Assumptions

This section defines the complete assumption space used for the Section 6
goals. Conditions already represented by the flow model are not additional
entries.

- **Disclosure of A's long-term key.** `K_as` is an independent variable
  data-exposure dimension. Its values are no attacker access and disclosure to
  the attacker from the beginning of the run.
- **Disclosure of B's long-term key.** `K_bs` is a second variable
  data-exposure dimension with the same values: no attacker access and
  disclosure from the beginning of the run.

The analysis uses the baseline, disclosure of `K_as` alone, and disclosure of
`K_bs` alone.

## 6  Security Properties

Exactly five goals are posed for formal verification. Every authentication
goal uses the tuple `(A, B, K_ab)`.

- **Session-key secrecy.** `K_ab` is secret from the attacker and may be known
  only by `A`, `B`, and `S`.
- **B's non-injective agreement with A.** `B` is the asserter and `A` is the
  subject of non-injective agreement on `(A, B, K_ab)`.
- **S's non-injective agreement with A.** `S` is the asserter and `A` is the
  subject of non-injective agreement on `(A, B, K_ab)`.
- **B's injective agreement with A.** `B` is the asserter and `A` is the
  subject of injective agreement on `(A, B, K_ab)`.
- **S's injective agreement with A.** `S` is the asserter and `A` is the
  subject of injective agreement on `(A, B, K_ab)`.

Session-key secrecy and B's non-injective agreement are evaluated in all three
scenarios. S's non-injective agreement is evaluated in the baseline and when
`K_as` alone is disclosed, but not when `K_bs` is disclosed. Both injective
agreement goals are evaluated only in the baseline.
